## Problem

A haptic interface lets software decide what a physical knob *feels* like: a spring that pulls back to centre, a hard
wall, a heavy flywheel, or click-stops like a volume dial. Making that convincing on a small microcontroller is
a real-time control problem. The loop has to sense, compute and actuate fast enough, and with stable enough timing,
that a stiff virtual wall doesn't buzz or go unstable.

## Approach

<EcsDiagram />

- **Peripherals in C.** The firmware integrates the quadrature decoder (FTM), ADC, PWM, the periodic interrupt timer
  (LPIT), UART and FlexCAN, each behind a small C interface.
- **One interrupt closes the loop.** At 1 kHz the ISR reads the wheel angle, estimates velocity by finite
  difference, evaluates the selected world and writes a PWM duty cycle. Nothing else is allowed to block it.
- **Worlds as torque laws.** Springs and walls are stiffness laws. Dampers add a velocity term. The spring-mass world
  integrates a virtual mass coupled to the wheel by a spring, so the knob feels like it drags a flywheel. The detent
  world applies a periodic restoring torque every 45°.
- **Model-based design.** The same worlds were modelled in Simulink/Stateflow and deployed as auto-generated C. Two
  boards were then networked over **CAN** to exchange state.

The worlds range from a soft centring spring to a wall ten times stiffer, plus damped, inertial and detent variants,
all rendered by the same 1 kHz interrupt.

## What I learned

- **Stiffness is limited by the loop, not the math.** A virtual wall can only be as stiff as sample rate, encoder
  resolution and velocity noise allow. That is why the wall world also has a damped variant.
- **Discretisation choices are physics choices.** How the spring-mass world integrates its state at 1 kHz decides
  whether the virtual flywheel gains or loses energy.
- **Hand-written vs generated code.** Writing the drivers by hand made the generated Simulink code easy to trust and
  debug, because I knew exactly what sat underneath it.
