## Problem

Microfluidic devices often need a flow rate that stays steady while the driving pressure varies. Usually that takes
active control. This project studied a **passive** alternative: a valve whose core is a soft porous material. As
pressure compresses it, its pores close and its hydraulic resistance rises, so the valve regulates itself. Designing
one requires knowing the material's pore structure, how its permeability changes with strain, and how fast it
relaxes.

- **Pore sizes from OCT.** I built the image-processing pipeline: surface extraction, local (adaptive) thresholding
  and cleanup. Its pore-size distributions came within **3.7 % NRMSE**. I swept block size and cleanup radius to show
  the result is stable, not tuned to one setting.
- **A cleaner flow rig.** I designed the fixture in CAD and assembled the setup, raising measurement SNR by **6 dB**.
  I then ran permeability experiments across a sweep of compressions, discarding pressure-step transients before
  fitting.
- **Models.** I proposed a flow-rate–pressure model based on the material's deformation profile, and isolated the
  poroviscoelastic time constant from indentation relaxation tests (the APS DFD contribution).

The work contributed to *A Self-Regulating Microfluidic Valve Based on Deformable Porous Media* (IEEE MEMS 2026,
Salzburg) and *Characterizing the Poroelastic Time Scale of a Poroviscoelastic Medium using Indentation Tests*
(APS DFD 2025, Houston).

## What I learned

- **Validate the measurement before the model.** The rig redesign (+6 dB) changed which effects were even visible in
  the data.
- **Prove a vision pipeline is stable.** Sensitivity sweeps over the free parameters is more convincing than one
  good-looking segmentation.
- **Experimental physics needs software discipline.** Separating preprocessing (unit conversion, transient removal)
  from analysis made every figure reproducible from raw logs.
