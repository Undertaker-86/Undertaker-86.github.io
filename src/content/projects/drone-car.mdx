## Problem

Most robots do one kind of locomotion well. A ground robot is efficient but stops at the first obstacle it can't
climb, and a drone can go anywhere but burns its battery just staying up. Inspired by Caltech's M4 Morphobot, we
built one robot that **drives on its wheels and turns those same wheels into rotors** when it needs to fly over
something. Making one set of hardware do both jobs is the hard part.

## Approach

- **One part, two jobs.** The outside of each 3D-printed propeller duct carries a rubber tread, so the duct is also the
  tyre. Servos tilt the pods between flat (wheels) and upright (rotors), and rare-earth magnets snap them into place.
- **Flight controller from scratch (my part).** I wrote the firmware myself: a complementary filter fuses accelerometer and gyro
  at 1 kHz, feeding a cascaded attitude PID and motor mixing. The PID is tuned for a mass distribution that changes
  between modes. Telemetry is non-blocking so the serial link can never stall the control loop.
- **Bench-tested before integration.** A load-cell thrust stand compared 8″ and 9″ propellers. We chose the 8″ for
  its faster spool-up, which attitude control needs, at the cost of some peak thrust.

## Results

- **Transformation works.** A full flat-to-upright cycle takes **about 10 s**, and the magnetic locks give a firm,
  audible snap and keep the pods aligned.
- **Driving works.** The robot held **over 0.8 m/s** across a 15 m straight, and a tethered sled-tow test confirmed
  the treads grip on tile and concrete.
- **Flight was marginal, and that is the main lesson.** Bare, each motor makes > 1 kg of thrust. With duct and tread it
  drops to ~850 g, or **3.4 kg total for a 2.5 kg robot**. That is enough to lift off, but the treads and metal
  hubs sit far out on the arms. The resulting rotational inertia left too little control margin, so the robot hopped
  and oscillated instead of hovering.

## What I learned

- **Mass and inertia budgets come first.** Each part that made the robot tougher on the ground made it worse in the
  air. A next version would use micro servos, a carbon-tube frame and lighter treads, and budget inertia rather
  than only mass.
- **Real-time firmware is about what you don't do in the loop.** At 1 kHz, a blocking serial print is a crash. Timing
  discipline mattered as much as the control law.
- **Integration surfaces the unmodelled.** Parts that worked alone interfered once assembled: motor current noise
  corrupted I²C sensor data. Power and signal isolation have to be designed in from the start.
